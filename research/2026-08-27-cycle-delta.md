## Override-worthy this cycle

1. **Age assurance just got a number. A US court settlement — not a regulator — is about to define what "working" means: a false-positive rate no higher than 3% for 13–15s and 10% for 16–17s, independently tested, audited for ten years.** On **2026-08-26**, eight days into trial in the **Northern District of California**, Meta agreed a proposed settlement with a **bipartisan coalition of 51 attorneys general** worth **up to $17 billion over ten years** (California alone taking **$1.5bn–$2.1bn**), subject to court approval by consent judgment. The money is not the finding. The **injunctive terms** are. Meta must institute age assurance to detect under-18s and to **identify and remove under-13s**, using **both its own and certified third-party tools**, with an **independent auditor holding expansive access and reporting regularly to the attorneys general**. As reported by Biometric Update and AP-syndicated coverage, the standard is **subject to independent testing** and must hit a **false positive rate of no more than 3% for ages 13–15 and 10% for ages 16–17**, with **Meta's proprietary systems required to match certified third-party accuracy within two years**. Two structural details do more work than the headline: **for the first 14 days after registration, every new user is treated as a teen regardless of stated age** — which closes the self-declared-birthdate loophole by making the lie temporarily worthless; and **if a user is removed as under-13, Meta must check the ages of their friends** — which treats the social graph itself as an age signal. Account: (primary) / company. Angle: this is the moment age assurance stops being a policy debate and becomes **a measured engineering specification with a published error budget**. Every age-assurance obligation the corpus holds — the UK Online Safety Act, Australia's eSafety regime, New Zealand's bill from two days ago — creates a *duty*. None of them says how accurate is accurate enough. **A settlement between a company and 51 attorneys general just did, and it did it in the one jurisdiction with no federal age-verification statute at all.** POV hook: "Every age-assurance law written so far tells platforms they must check. None of them says what counts as checking well. This week a court settlement did what the legislatures didn't: it put an error budget on it. Three percent for 13-to-15s. Ten percent for 16-to-17s. Independently tested, audited for a decade. Regulators write duties. Litigation writes specifications — and specifications are what vendors actually get procured against." Second hook, sharper and more honest: **the 14-day rule is the real innovation.** Treating every new account as a minor for two weeks is an admission that age assurance at the point of registration does not work well enough to rely on, so the system defaults to safe and re-decides later. That is **progressive trust** — the same architecture the corpus has argued for continuously in `identity-orchestration.md` Sub-theme B, arriving in a consumer platform because the alternative was a jury. Constraints: **use the California AG's "$17 billion over ten years / 51 attorneys general" figure**, not the $18bn secondary framing (~30% of which is contingent on TikTok and YouTube matching); **the FPR thresholds are secondary-sourced and absent from the AG release — attribute as reported, not confirmed**; **this is a proposed settlement pending judicial approval, not a final judgment**; Meta denies liability; **the US is not a named Ditto ICP geography and social media is not a Ditto vertical** — use as category evidence for the audited-effectiveness thesis, never as a market claim; and **do not imply the thresholds apply to gambling, banking or any other age-gated sector** — they do not, yet.

2. **A US federal identity platform is now asking vendors to detect and classify ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini agents by name — because "is this a human" has stopped being a safe assumption at the login box.** On **2026-08-26** the **General Services Administration** issued a **request for information** seeking device-intelligence and fingerprinting capability for **Login.gov**, with responses due **2026-09-11**. The RFI asks for three things: **persistent device recognition** at both account creation and sign-in, explicitly required to **recognise a returning device even when the user evades detection via VPNs, residential proxies, incognito browsing, cookie clearing or browser tampering**; **detection and classification of bots and agentic AI, naming large-language-model agents from ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini**, with adaptability built in for agents that do not exist yet; and **real-time risk signals for account takeover, synthetic identity fraud, automated bot activity and residential proxy abuse**. GSA states the **sophistication and volume of fraud attempts have grown as the user base and agency adoption expanded**, already uses fingerprinting inside identity proofing, and says it **prefers a specialist provider over a full platform** unless more components prove necessary. Account: (primary) / company. Angle: this is the first item in the corpus where **a government identity system treats named commercial AI agents as a traffic class to be identified at the front door**, and it inverts the entire agentic-identity conversation the corpus has been tracking since Experian's Agent Operating System (06-04). Every framework so far — MCP-I, Know Your Agent, Ping's NHI argument, InterSAGE's four-layer model, Microsoft Entra Agent ID — asks *how do we give agents credentials so they can act legitimately*. GSA is asking the prior question: *how do we tell that this is an agent at all, when it is deliberately trying not to look like one?* POV hook: "Every agentic-identity framework published this year answers the same question: how do we issue credentials to AI agents so they can act on someone's behalf. This week a US federal login system asked the question that comes before it — how do we even know it's an agent? Its RFI names ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini as traffic to be detected and classified, alongside residential proxies and account takeover. Delegation is the interesting problem. Detection is the urgent one, and almost nobody is funding it." Second angle, and the one that reaches `mobile-trust.md` Sub-theme B directly: **the requirement is persistence against active evasion.** A device signal that survives a VPN, a cleared cookie jar and a tampered browser is not a convenience feature — it is the only continuous identity signal in the stack that the user cannot simply reset, and it is precisely the signal the SEON World Cup data showed was missing when dormant verified accounts reactivated on unfamiliar devices. Constraints: **an RFI is market research, not a procurement or an award** — do not write "GSA is buying" or name any vendor as a likely winner; **do not imply Ditto responds to or is eligible for US federal RFIs**; Login.gov is a US-government system and the US is not a named ICP — this is category evidence about where identity assurance is heading, not a market signal. Note the awkward second-order point worth being honest about in any post: **fingerprinting a device persistently through a user's deliberate privacy measures is in real tension with the privacy-first, minimal-disclosure architecture Ditto argues for.** The strongest version of this post names that tension rather than ignoring it.
