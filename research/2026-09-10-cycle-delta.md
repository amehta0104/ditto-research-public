## Override-worthy this cycle

1. **US federal banking regulators have just told every bank and credit union in the country that a cryptographically signed digital credential counts as government-issued ID for opening an account — in person or fully remote.** FinCEN, the Federal Reserve, FDIC, NCUA and OCC issued joint FAQs on 2026-09-08 defining a verifiable digital credential as "a data structure containing information about an individual, digitally signed by its issuer, cryptographically bound to a device, and protected by an activation factor." Account: / company. Angle: for years the answer to "can we accept a cryptographic credential instead of a photo of a licence?" was *ask your regulator*. Five of them have now answered, together, in writing. The guidance neither requires nor prohibits it — which means the constraint has moved from the regulator to the bank's own stack. **The question is no longer whether you may accept a VDC. It is whether you can verify one.**

Account: company /. Angle: this lands **one day** after Microsoft shipped the Windows Age API. One OS vendor built it voluntarily; another government is now compelling it. The platform layer is being conscripted as identity infrastructure, and every app that currently runs its own age gate is about to find out whether it is a compliance control or a redundant one.

3. **A certified age-assurance provider has now actually left a European market rather than weaken its own security model — the exit took effect today.** From 2026-09-10 the app is off Spanish app stores, following an AEPD resolution of 2026-03-10 that fined €500,000 for Article 9 biometric processing, €200,000 for invalid consent and €250,000 for excessive retention. ## New findings

### Pillar 1: Banking & Payments

- **Five US federal financial regulators have jointly confirmed that state-issued mobile driver's licences and other government-issued verifiable digital credentials satisfy the Customer Identification Program rule under the Bank Secrecy Act — including for accounts opened entirely remotely.** **FinCEN, the Federal Reserve, the FDIC, the NCUA and the OCC** published two new FAQs and amended one existing FAQ on **2026-09-08**. The operative findings: an **"unexpired government-issued VDC, such as an mDL, qualifies as 'government-issued identification'"** for CIP purposes; institutions **"may accept an mDL when opening an account in person or remotely via the Internet or another digital or virtual channel"**; and a VDC is defined as **"a data structure containing information about an individual, digitally signed by its issuer, cryptographically bound to a device, and protected by an activation factor"** (PIN, password or biometric). Three constraints matter as much as the permission. **(a)** The rule **"neither requires nor prohibits"** reliance on VDCs — it is risk-based, and the FAQs **do not create new supervisory expectations or change BSA/CIP requirements**. **(b)** Institutions must **"maintain the appropriate technology or systems to extract the relevant information"** — the regulators have permitted a credential most banks cannot currently read. **(c)** Where a credential **shows indications of fraud, that must be weighed** in forming the reasonable belief that the institution knows the customer's true identity — so acceptance does not transfer risk. **For an identity vendor this is the most consequential US banking finding in the corpus.** It converts mDL acceptance from a state-DMV curiosity into a federally-blessed onboarding path, and it does so in language that is credential-format-neutral: *digitally signed, device-bound, activation-protected*. That is a specification, not a brand. **It also resolves the NIST SP 1800-42 watch thread from the 09-08 delta** — the supervisory answer arrived before the NIST implementation guidance did.

### Pillar 7: Fraud / Deepfakes

- **Ofcom has opened an enforcement programme requiring UK platforms to have automated hash-matching against non-consensual intimate imagery and explicit AI deepfakes running by 30 September, and has signalled a 48-hour statutory takedown obligation to follow.** Announced **2026-09-09** under the strengthened **Illegal Content Codes of Practice** of the **Online Safety Act**, the programme states that **"covered services should have hash-matching technology in place by September 30"** — converting an image to **"a numerical digital fingerprint"** compared against fingerprints of known abusive material. Ofcom recommends **StopNCII**, whose design lets a victim **generate the hash on their own device** so the image itself never leaves it. Ofcom **"may take enforcement action when a service fails to meet its legal duties"**, and a **separate consultation before the end of 2026** will propose requiring removal of reported NCII **within 48 hours**. **The identity-relevant point is the architecture, not the enforcement.** StopNCII is client-side hashing: the system proves a match without ever receiving the underlying sensitive artefact. **That is the same privacy property as attribute-only age disclosure and the same property as proving possession of a credential without transmitting it — a regulator has now mandated it as the default for the most sensitive content class there is.** When a buyer asks why verification should not retain the document, this is the precedent: the UK regulator's own recommended design for the most sensitive material in scope is one where the sensitive artefact never moves.

### Pillar 9: Passwordless / split-key

- **Singapore has extended national-identity passkeys to Android, three months after iOS, with roughly 800,000 citizens enrolled and desktop browser support due by year end.** **GovTech** began the phased **Singpass** Android rollout on **2026-09-09**, following the June 2026 iPhone deployment. Private keys stay in local device hardware and the user authenticates with on-device fingerprint or face, **"allowing the device to prove possession without transmitting a password or one-time code that a scammer could intercept."** The stated driver is phishing — the **second most common scam type in Singapore in 2025** per Singapore Police Force data cited by GovTech, at **S$39.9 million in reported losses**. **Crucially, passkeys are an additional option, not a replacement**: QR login, face verification and **SMS OTP all remain available**. **That last detail is the finding, and it cuts against the vendor narrative.** A sophisticated national identity system with 800,000 passkey enrolments has **not** retired the OTP, because the fallback is the accessibility floor. **The phishing-resistance of a passkey is bounded by whatever the account recovery path allows** — an attacker does not defeat the passkey, they choose the SMS. First substantive P9 finding since the CIMB SecureTAC sunset, and the two make a natural pair: one national ID system adding a phishing-resistant option while keeping the phishable one, one bank actually retiring theirs on **2026-09-19**.
